📘 Detailed Correction – 9th – In-depth Analysis

I- Reading Comprehension (6 marks) – Correction & Analysis

1. Tick the right option: 1 mark

Answer:

The text shows that Sara thinks shopping online is safe if you are careful. She explicitly says: "I am very careful with all of my personal details" and "It is important to be sure that the web page is secure." She acknowledges that "bad things sometimes happen" but she mitigates the risk through cautious behaviour.

🔍 In-depth Analysis: This question tests the ability to distinguish nuanced opinion from absolute statements. Sara does not claim online shopping is "always safe" (option b) because she admits "bad things sometimes happen." Nor does she say it is "often dangerous" (option a) — she downplays the danger and emphasises her own precautions. The correct answer requires understanding the conditional safety she describes: safety depends on being careful. The key lexical signals are "but I am very careful" (contrastive conjunction introducing a personal safeguard) and "It is important to be sure that the web page is secure" (impersonal construction expressing a general precautionary principle). This is a Level 2 comprehension skill: interpreting the author's stance rather than merely locating explicit facts.

2. Why does Sara prefer online shopping? 1 mark

Answer:

Sara prefers online shopping because it is convenient (she can shop from bed using her phone), it saves her a lot of time, and because she is busy and forgetful, she appreciates being able to buy something the moment she thinks of it. She also mentions that items arrive quickly ("Yesterday, I bought a birthday present for my mother, and it arrived today!").

🔍 In-depth Analysis: This question requires synthesising information from multiple paragraphs. Paragraph (1) highlights convenience and comfort: "more convenient," "from bed." Paragraph (3) emphasises time-saving and immediacy: "That saves me a lot of time," "I can buy it at that moment." The student must connect Sara's personal traits ("very busy," "a little forgetful") to her shopping preference — online shopping compensates for her lifestyle constraints. This is a cause-and-effect reading skill: recognising that a character's habits or personality traits explain their preferences. A strong answer will mention at least two reasons and support them with reference to the text, even if not directly quoted.

3. Correct the false statements with details from the text: 2 marks

Answers:

a) Sara does not buy everything online. She still prefers getting clothes from a store so she can try them on to be sure they fit her fine.
Correction: "But I still prefer getting clothes from a store so I can try them on to be sure they fit me fine."

b) Her friends are not comfortable with buying things online.
Correction: "My friends aren't as comfortable with buying things online as I am because they think that it can be dangerous."

🔍 In-depth Analysis: This exercise tests the ability to identify and rectify misinformation by close reading. Statement (a) is an overgeneralisation — the word "everything" is absolute and contradicted by the exception Sara makes for clothes. The student must locate the adversative conjunction "But" signalling the exception. Statement (b) is the inverse of the text's claim — the friends are explicitly "not as comfortable," which the student must convert from a comparative structure into a simple negative statement. The phrase "aren't as comfortable as I am" is a comparative of inequality that requires careful parsing: it means the friends are less comfortable, not that they are equally uncomfortable. This question also tests paraphrase skills, as the correction should rephrase the original text accurately.

4. Find a word meaning "to take something without permission": 1 mark

Answer:

steal (from paragraph 4: "someone will steal their number")

🔍 In-depth Analysis: This vocabulary-in-context question targets the word "steal," which appears in the final paragraph in the context of credit card fraud. The definition "to take something without permission" is a standard dictionary gloss of the verb. The student must scan the text for a word matching this semantic field (crime, appropriation). The surrounding context — "credit cards," "fear," "number" — reinforces the meaning. Recognising that "steal" applies to intangible items (a credit card number) as well as physical objects is part of the word's extended meaning. This question also subtly reinforces the theme of online safety that runs through the text.

5. What does "it" refer to? 1 mark

Answer:

"it" refers to the birthday present that Sara bought for her mother. (Paraphrase: the present she ordered online.)

🔍 In-depth Analysis: Pronoun reference resolution is a fundamental reading skill. In paragraph (3), the sentence "Yesterday, I bought a birthday present for my mother, and it arrived today!" features the pronoun "it" which anaphorically refers to the nearest preceding singular noun phrase: "a birthday present." The student must trace the referent across the coordinating conjunction "and." This seemingly simple exercise is crucial because misidentifying the referent could lead to confusion about what arrived — the mother? the day? The grammatical number (singular) and the semantic compatibility (a present can arrive; a mother cannot "arrive" in this context without additional specification) guide the correct interpretation.

II- Language (8 marks) – Correction & Grammar Rules

1- Circle the correct alternative. 3 marks

Answers:

• Because of his selfishness... (Because of + noun phrase indicates cause)
• doing harm to the environment. (do + harm is a collocation; "harmful" is an adjective, "harmless" means the opposite)
• to our planet. (first-person plural possessive, consistent with "we have done")
• very polluted. (past participle as adjective after "very")
• recycle waste. (logical action to save the earth; "dispose" and "throw" have negative connotations in this context)
• sources of energy which are less polluting. (relative pronoun for things)

✔ Grammar Justification:
• Because of + noun: prepositional phrase expressing cause; "Because" (conjunction) would require a clause, "That's why" introduces a result, "However" expresses contrast.
• do harm: fixed collocation; "harmful" is an adjective ("is harmful to"), "harmless" means "not causing harm" — the opposite of the intended meaning.
• our planet: the pronoun must agree with the subject "we" in the preceding clause ("what we have done to our planet").
• polluted: the past participle functions as an adjective describing the state of the environment; "pollution" is a noun, "polluting" is the present participle (active).
• recycle: the only environmentally positive verb among the options; "dispose" and "throw" imply discarding without reuse.
• which: relative pronoun for non-human antecedents ("sources of energy"); "who" is for people, "where" is for places.

2- Fill in the blanks with words from the list. 3 marks

Answers:

1. travelling (by train remains one favourite alternative)
2. reasons (many reasons for choosing this slower means)
3. convenient (more convenient and economical)
4. passenger (the passenger can enjoy the varied scenery)
5. greatest (the greatest advantage of train travel)
6. sightsee (opportunity to sightsee while chatting)

✔ Lexical & Cohesive Justification:
• travelling: gerund form functioning as subject of the sentence; "flying" would contradict "by train."
• reasons: collocates with "many" and the preposition "for" + gerund.
• convenient: paired with "economical" by the conjunction "and," both adjectives describing advantages.
• passenger: the person who travels; logical subject for "enjoy the scenery" and "moving about."
• greatest: superlative adjective fitting "the ... advantage" structure; signals the most important benefit.
• sightsee: verb meaning to visit interesting places; fits "opportunity to" + infinitive and "while chatting."

3- Put the words in the right tense or form. 2 marks

Answers:

1. have improved (present perfect: "Recently" signals a past action with present relevance)
2. newest (superlative adjective: "the ... phone")
3. addicted (adjective: "becoming addicted to")
4. distracting (gerund after "leads to": "leads to distracting them")

✔ Tense & Form Rules:
• Recently → present perfect: "Recently" is a time adverb typically used with the present perfect to indicate an action completed in the recent past with current relevance. "Have improved" (plural subject "phones, computers and other electronic devices").
• the + superlative: "the newest phone" — the definite article + superlative form of "new" (double the 'e': new → newer → newest).
• become + adjective: "addicted" is the adjectival form derived from the verb "addict." The expression is "be/become addicted to something."
• lead to + gerund: "leads to" is a phrasal verb that must be followed by a noun or gerund (-ing form). "Distracting" comes from the verb "distract" (note: the text has a typo "distruct" — the correct base is "distract"). The gerund "distracting them" functions as the object of the preposition "to."

III- Writing – Article (6 marks) – Plan, Model & Analysis

Task: Write an article about the dangers of smoking and convince people to give it up.

📌 Expected Article Structure

  • Engaging title: e.g., "Break Free: Your Life Is Worth More Than a Cigarette"
  • Introduction: startling fact or rhetorical question about smoking.
  • Body paragraph 1: the dangers of smoking (health, financial, social).
  • Body paragraph 2: practical steps to quit and reasons for hope.
  • Conclusion: powerful call to action, encouraging readers to stop.

📝 Model Article (expected level):

Break Free: Your Life Is Worth More Than a Cigarette Did you know that every cigarette you smoke steals about eleven minutes of your life? Smoking is not just a bad habit — it is a silent killer that destroys your health, empties your wallet, and hurts the people around you. If you have ever thought about quitting, now is the perfect time to take action. The dangers of smoking are no secret. Cigarettes contain over 7,000 chemicals, many of which are poisonous and cause cancer. Smoking damages your lungs, weakens your heart, and increases the risk of stroke. It does not only affect you: second-hand smoke puts your family and friends in danger too. Financially, smoking is like setting money on fire — imagine how much you could save in a year by giving up just one pack a day. Socially, smoking isolates you, as more and more public places become smoke-free and people grow less tolerant of the smell and the health risks. But there is hope. Thousands of people quit smoking every year, and so can you. Start by setting a quit date and telling your friends and family so they can support you. Replace the cigarette break with a healthy habit, like chewing sugar-free gum, drinking water, or taking a short walk. There are also apps, hotlines, and support groups that can guide you through the process. Remember: the first few days are the hardest, but every smoke-free day makes you stronger. Your body begins to repair itself within hours of your last cigarette. So, make the decision today. You owe it to yourself, to your loved ones, and to your future. Stub out that cigarette, take a deep breath of fresh air, and step into a healthier, happier life. You are stronger than you think — and a smoke-free you is the best version of you. — A friend who believes in your strength
✔ Key strengths of this model: The article opens with a shocking statistic to grab attention and immediately establishes the stakes. The body is divided into two clear sections: the first details the dangers (health, financial, social) with specific examples, and the second provides actionable advice for quitting, balancing warning with encouragement. The tone is persuasive yet supportive — it does not shame the reader but empowers them. Connectives ("But," "So," "Remember") ensure logical flow. The conclusion is an urgent, uplifting call to action with the memorable image of taking "a deep breath of fresh air." The language is appropriate for a school magazine: accessible, vivid, and grammatically varied.

Marking criteria – Writing (6 marks):
• Adherence to task & content (2 marks): clear article format, all points addressed, persuasive purpose.
• Lexical appropriacy & grammar accuracy (2 marks): varied vocabulary, correct tenses, sentence structures.
• Mechanical accuracy (2 marks): spelling, punctuation, paragraphing.
